Devlog.

Notes from working on the game, written up from what actually happened. Bugs and wrong turns included.

13 Sept 2026

Getting to orbit under power

Up until this week, getting a ship into orbit meant doing it the way a real rocket does it: a burn, a long coast, another burn. That's about an hour of sitting there for a transfer to the Moon. Fine if you're a chemical rocket with no fuel to spare. Not fine for a ship that can hold two g of thrust indefinitely, which is what the reactors in this game do.

So I changed how the autopilot thinks about it. Instead of planning burns, at every tick it works out the velocity the ship should have to get where it's going, compares that to the velocity it actually has, and points the nose at the difference. Gravity just shows up as error every tick and the throttle keeps correcting for it, which means the ship effectively hovers when it needs to. One rule ends up covering capturing into orbit, raising, lowering, and circularising.

Result: a ship with real thrust goes from 170 km above the Moon to a clean orbit in under four minutes, with the actual physics running the whole way. Eccentricity comes out flat to four decimal places. If you cut the engine at any point you're on a real orbit and the orbit panel draws it.

The last bug took a live trace to find. The ship would get close, overshoot, correct, overshoot, and swing its nose about 140 degrees every cycle, forever. The braking math was never quite reaching zero where it needed to. Fix was one line.

13 Sept 2026

Ship classes, on paper

Wrote up how ships are going to get classified. Not built yet, just designed. The idea is the game looks at the ship and decides what it is, rather than the player picking from a list. Whatever system takes up the biggest share of the ship's dry mass sets the type: hauler, miner, warship, scout, carrier. The second biggest becomes the adjective, so you'd get an armoured hauler or a long-range miner. Size sets the class within the type, and the class names are different per type. A hauler's biggest size is a Titan, a warship's is a Dreadnought. Civilian ships never get military names.

The number I care about is rated acceleration at full load, because that doesn't change as cargo comes and goes. From that you get a lift ceiling, the strongest gravity the ship can leave fully loaded. A big capital ship that can only manage orbit isn't failing anything, that's what it's for.

One thing I want to get right from the start: the classifier only looks at a set of features, never at the ship object itself. So the same code can rate your own blueprint and whatever an enemy's scanner picks up. A smuggler flying a warship dressed up as a hauler doesn't need a special system. It's just a ship whose scanned features don't match its paperwork.

12 Sept 2026

Landing bug finally fixed

About two weeks ago the first landing test came back with: you sink into the ground, catch on something invisible, and slide. Finding out why took rebuilding how terrain works.

The terrain part first. Planets used to be voxel data that only really mattered up close. Now a planet is one continuous surface that refines from orbit all the way down to a vertex every 5 meters, and collision is built on the finest patches directly. Standing on the Moon, the surface renders in a fraction of the time the old system needed, and a raycast lands on it to the millimeter. Rendering, collision and the mining math are all reading the same surface now, which they weren't before.

That's what exposed the actual bug. Two numbers in two different files were supposed to match and didn't. One system parked your feet slightly above the ground as a safety margin. Another system only counted you as landed if you were slightly below that. So you'd fall, get caught, get placed just out of reach of the landed check, and fall again, over and over, sliding the whole time. Sixteen days, two files, one line.

9 Sept 2026

Sensors

Designed the sensor system on Tuesday and had the first version working Wednesday, which is unusual.

The starting point is that there's no real stealth in space. So instead of a stealth mode there's a budget. Every ship is spending against four things at once: heat against the cold of deep space, reflected light, radio noise, and how big a radar return its shape gives off. You don't hide. You spend less.

On the seeing side, sensors are one wavelength dial instead of separate modes. Slide it from visible light to radar or thermal infrared and each planet reads differently. A hot planet's surface glows straight through its own clouds in the near infrared. A world that's a flat disc in one band has real contours in another.

Getting the look right took a rewrite. The first pass looked like security camera footage, scanlines and grain and static, and I threw it out the same day. It needed to look like an instrument that works, not a broken TV. What's in now is a processed signal: colour ramps, contour lines at even signal levels, edges pulled out of the data, one slow sweep every 17 seconds.

9 Sept 2026

Mercury terrain

Every other body either had real elevation data or an obvious placeholder. Mercury had neither. It was a uniformly wrinkled ball because nobody had touched its terrain settings since the surface system was new, and by nobody I mean me.

Pulled in the real elevation model from the MESSENGER mission. After converting it I checked it against named landmarks, which is worth doing, because a mirrored map or a wrong roll looks completely fine until you compare it to something real. The biggest basin reads out as the lowest point on the planet, which is correct.

Getting there meant rewriting the tool that reads these files, since elevation data doesn't come in one consistent format. Testing it against sixty real files found three separate bugs, none of which crashed anything, they just quietly made a wrong but plausible looking planet. A byte-order mistake, a wrong assumption about number formats, and a signed/unsigned mixup that turns every basin into a mountain.

7 Sept 2026

Earth and the Sun got a proper shader pass

For most of the project the planets have been lit correctly but never looked like much. Flat colour, no atmosphere, the Sun was a bright dot. This week Earth got the full treatment.

Day and night blend across the terminator, city lights come up at dusk, the ocean actually glints, and there's a separate atmosphere layer that glows at the rim the way real air scattering does. It's set up to extend: one shader for every planet's ground, one for atmospheres, per-planet materials on top. The Sun changed too. Not a big hazy glow, a tight bright core with a few sharp rays and a slow flicker. Closer to a photo than a lens flare.

None of this changes gameplay at all. It's just the difference between a solar system that's technically correct and one that looks like somewhere you'd want to go.

29 Aug 2026

The Sun had no gravity

For a while, once you left Earth's sphere of influence, physics went with it. Past a certain distance the ship just flew in a straight line. No gravity, no orbit panel, no manoeuvres. It looked like a missing feature. It was one reversed comparison.

The function that decides which body owns a ship picks the smallest sphere of influence that contains you. The Sun's sphere of influence is, correctly, infinite. Infinity is not smaller than infinity. So the Sun passed the "am I inside this" check and then lost the comparison every time, and interplanetary space had no dominant body because the one body big enough to claim it got disqualified by its own size.

Found it the boring way, printing what the function actually returned at five different altitudes instead of reasoning about what it should return. There's now a rule against comparing a value to itself when infinity is involved, and tests to catch it if it comes back.

29 Aug 2026

Target lock

Auto-orbit worked right up until it didn't know what you meant. Tell the ship to circularise with nothing selected and it would circularise around the Sun. Technically correct, useless if you meant Earth.

Fixed with a target lock: an actual selected object, locked in the moment you give the order, so changing your mind mid-burn doesn't quietly redirect a burn that's already running. Four manoeuvres came with it, point at target, match velocity, approach, hold station, plus two cockpit screens built around whatever's locked.

The sneaky bug in this one: relative velocity between two ships has to be measured in one shared frame, not by comparing two numbers that are each relative to a different planet. Get that wrong and your own ship can read as standing still with a real error hiding in the number. It looks fine as long as everything's near one planet, which is exactly why it's easy to miss.

29 Aug 2026

Voice input, first pass

Say "ship, burn retrograde" out loud and it shows up as text on screen. That's the whole feature for now, on purpose. This pass is just the microphone and the transcript. Hooking the words up to actual commands is next.

Getting here meant backing out of a decision I'd already made. The original plan used a voice engine that advertised understanding a small fixed set of commands directly. Turned out that wasn't true once I actually tried it in the engine instead of trusting the README. What's running now is a local speech model, bundled into the project and trimmed to less than half its original size, so nothing upstream going away can break the build.

The one rule that mattered more than which engine: the microphone is off by default, no exceptions. A game that quietly listens is exactly the kind of thing that loses people's trust. The mic light and level meter on screen are tied to whether the microphone is actually feeding audio in, not just whether the feature is switched on.
