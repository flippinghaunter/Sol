What it is

Sol is a ship-building space game. You put a ship together block by block on a grid, run cables between the reactor and the things that need power, and then you walk down the corridor, sit in the pilot seat, and take it somewhere. The somewhere is the actual solar system. Every planet and every major moon is there at its real size, on its real orbit, and you can land on any of the rocky ones and get out and walk around.

I'm making it because I wanted the middle ground between Space Engineers and Kerbal Space Program. Space Engineers has the building and the systems but the world is small and made up. KSP has the real orbital mechanics but you don't live in your ship. Sol is trying to have both, and to do it in a solar system that's the real one.

The one rule that sits above everything else: it's a game with the theme of scientific accuracy, not a simulation. The physics is real. Gravity is real, orbits are real, distances are real. But the numbers that would make you sit and wait, or pull out a calculator, get bent. Reactors and thrusters are sci-fi strong, so a ship flies to the orbit it wants in a few minutes instead of coasting for an hour. There's a jump drive for the long trips. There's an autopilot that does the orbital mechanics for you, and it's meant to be good enough that most people never fly by hand. If you want to fly by hand, you can, and the sim underneath will hold up.

What doesn't bend is time. There's no time warp and there won't be one, because the plan is for this to be a shared world eventually, and you can't fast-forward a world that other people are in.

How it plays

Building

Blocks go on a grid and can sit in any of 24 orientations. There are shaped blocks, not just cubes, so hulls can actually look like hulls. Bigger structures are made from lattice frames that you skin with panels. Cables are placed by hand and form real networks. If a reactor isn't cabled to a thruster, the thruster doesn't fire. Blueprints let you save a design and put it back down.

Mass is real and comes from the blocks you used. Where the mass sits matters. Damage is structural: blocks take hits, and if a hit cuts a ship in two along its actual connections, you get two ships. The cables on the wrong half stop working.

Flying

You sit in a seat and the ship's computer flies. You tell it what you want through three screens in the cockpit: a pilot screen for doing things, a status screen for finding things out, and a navigation and sensors screen for knowing what's around you. Orbit, radar, contacts, target, power, thrusters each have a panel. Manual control is always there, but the normal way to play is to point at something and say go.

The autopilot will get you into orbit, change orbit, match another ship, hold station, approach, and land. When it can't do what you asked, it tells you why in plain words, like "cannot take off from the surface, add thrust or drop cargo," instead of showing you two numbers and letting you work it out. The idea is that you should never have to compare numbers to play this game.

Thrust-to-weight is the stat that matters. If your ship is slow, that's a ship problem, and you fix it by bolting on more thrusters or a bigger reactor.

Getting around

For the long distances there's a jump drive. You keep your velocity through a jump, so arriving somewhere is a burn to slow down, not a wait. The nav map shows the whole system. Right now jumping is free; eventually it will cost fuel or charge or heat, that's not designed yet.

On the ground

Every rocky planet and moon has a surface you can land on and walk on under its own gravity. Where NASA has real elevation data (the Moon, Mercury, Mars, and so on) that's what the terrain is built from. Everywhere else it's generated from a seed so it's always the same. The Moon's terrain goes down to a vertex every 5 meters when you're standing on it.

Seeing things

Sensors work as a wavelength dial rather than separate modes. Slide it from visible light to infrared or radar and things look different. Venus's surface shows through its clouds in the near infrared. Light takes time to travel, so what you see of something far away is where it was, not where it is. Stealth isn't a mode either. Every ship gives off heat, reflects light, makes radio noise, and has a radar shape, and hiding means spending less on all four at once.

Voice

You can talk to the ship. It runs a local speech model, so nothing goes to a server, and the microphone is off unless you turn it on. Right now it transcribes what you say. Wiring the words to actual commands is next.

The world
32real bodies with gravity and orbits: the Sun, 8 planets, 20 major moons, Ceres, Pluto, Charon
384real asteroids over 80 km, on their real orbits, from NASA's small-body database
~13Mprocedural asteroids in the main belt, Kuiper belt, Trojans, Hildas, Centaurs and near-Earth space

The procedural asteroids are a seeded density field, so a rock only exists in memory when you're near it, but it's the same rock in the same place every time you come back. The big named ones, Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and the rest, are hand-built, including what's in them to mine.

Planets have real albedo and, where the maps were available, real colour. Earth has day and night, city lights, an atmosphere that glows at the edge. That kind of visual pass is only done for Earth and the Sun so far; the rest are lit correctly but plain.

Where it's going

The loop is build, fly, land or dock, do something out there (mine, trade, fight), and come back and build something better. Early on that's you and one small ship working the near-Earth asteroids. Mid game you've founded a corporation and you've got haulers and drones running routes while you're off doing something else. Late game is terraforming, which is where all the resources in the system end up going.

Mining and resources

Mining lasers cut volume out of an asteroid and it shows up as ore in a container. A refinery turns ore into metal, an assembler turns metal into block stock, and blocks cost stock to place. Ice feeds life support and fuel. Later, a mining outpost eats a body over time at whatever rate its machinery allows. Cargo has mass, so a loaded hauler flies like a loaded hauler.

Corporations and the economy

Player corporations and NPC corporations are the same kind of thing. NPC ones have personalities and run on a simulation tick with real travel distances, so a hauler from Mars takes as long as a hauler from Mars would. You hire pilots, give them standing orders, and copy your best blueprint out of a shipyard as many times as you can afford. Stations post contracts. Raids happen and you place turrets. Corporations can go bankrupt, get bought, merge. There's a leaderboard.

Life support and the inside of the ship

Rooms will be sealed volumes. Doors will actually open and close, airlocks will cycle, and a hull breach will vent the room. You'll have suit oxygen. Ships will have rotors, hinges and pistons so you can build things that move, and material tubes so ore and ammunition can flow around the hull.

Terraforming

This is the end game. Every body has a percentage, and corporations contribute to it. The jump drive will be able to move small bodies, so you can anchor an asteroid and park it in orbit or drop it on a planet and get credit for the mass. There's a machine called the World Engine that holds an atmosphere in place and heats or cools it, and it decays if you stop powering it. Ceres is the first body I'm going to work through. Land on a terraformed body gets parcelled out to whoever contributed.

Multiplayer

Single player first. Then one player hosts and friends join. Then hosted servers with a shared, persistent world where the corporations keep running when you're logged off. The architecture is being kept open for that from the start, which is why there's no time warp and why the NPC economy is built as a ledger rather than as ships flying around.

The rest

Weather on the gas giants and Venus and Mars. Flying down into an atmosphere and having the clouds you saw from orbit become real formations around you. The Sun's prominences big enough to fly under. Radar and thermal and radio as different ways of being seen, and stealth as the cost of not being seen. Ships getting auto-classified as hauler, miner, warship, scout or carrier based on what they actually are. Probes and drones on a smaller block scale. Sound. Effects. A tutorial. Steam.

Under the hood
Engine
Godot 4.5, GDScript, static typing everywhere.
Scale
Double-precision positions, a floating origin, and body-relative frames, so the whole solar system fits without the ground jittering under your feet.
Terrain
Cube-sphere quadtree with geomorphing. Real elevation data (LOLA, MESSENGER) where it exists, procedural detail on top.
Testing
About 800 automated checks run headless on every change. Every new check gets mutation-tested before I trust it.
Data
All real-world data is credited by source: NASA, USGS, Solar System Scope (CC BY), Poly Haven (CC0).
Balance
Every number that might need tuning is an exported dial. Balancing happens later by turning dials, not by rewriting code.
